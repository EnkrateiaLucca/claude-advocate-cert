D1

Prompting and Task Execution

Exam weight 14%

Build prompts from explicit components, decompose multi-part work, diagnose weak output and adapt your strategy to the task type.

Q1
What are the five components of a well-built prompt?
  1. Role
  2. Context (including audience)
  3. Task
  4. Constraints
  5. Output format
Figure · Weak vs. strong prompt
Weak prompt: everything left implicit "Write a summary of our quarterly operations."

Claude returns three plausible paragraphs that could describe almost any company. No audience, figures, format or priorities. The output is not wrong. It is unusable, because the prompt specified almost nothing.

Strong prompt: components made explicit "You are an operations analyst (role). I am preparing a one-page update for our regional director, who cares about throughput and cost, not process detail (context and audience). Summarize the attached Q3 operations data (task), covering only the three metrics that moved more than 10 percent against target (constraint). Format as a short headline followed by three bullet points, each one sentence (output format)."
Q2
A communications manager must turn a dense 20-page policy change into a staff announcement, a staff FAQ and a short executive briefing. Decompose this into an ordered sequence of steps to run with Claude.
  1. Extract the substantive changes and what each one means in practice.
  2. Confirm the extraction is complete and accurate before building on it.
  3. Draft the staff announcement from the confirmed change list, tuned to a general audience.
  4. Draft the FAQ, anticipating the questions staff will ask.
  5. Draft the executive briefing, compressed to decisions and impact.

Key idea: verify the shared foundation once, then build every deliverable on it.

Q5
A draft is close but misses on one section. What should you do?
Iterate on that section only. Do not discard a draft that is mostly right.
Source: Prompt diagnostics
Worked example · A live iteration cycle
Round 1 prompt"Write a follow-up email to the client about the delayed deliverable."
Round 1 outputA generic, slightly defensive three-paragraph email that never says when the deliverable will arrive or why it slipped. Diagnosis: thin context (no reason, no new date) and no tone constraint.
Round 2 prompt"Write a follow-up to the client about the two-day delay on the analytics deliverable. The cause was a data-quality issue we have now fixed; new delivery is Thursday. Tone: accountable, not over-apologetic. Keep it under 120 words."
Round 2 outputA tight, accountable note with the cause, the new date and a confident close. Diagnosis: strong. Only the subject line is missing.
Round 3 prompt"Good. Add a subject line that signals resolution, not just delay."

The lesson: fix the specific component the output exposed. Do not restart from scratch.

Q6
What are the four core task types for everyday chat use of Claude?
Analysis · Research · Drafting · Brainstorming
Q7
When you set up an analysis prompt, what three things do you spell out?
What to measure, the standard to measure it against and how to handle ambiguity. Low creative latitude, high specification.
Source: Prompt configuration by task type
Q8
When you set up a research prompt, what do you define up front and what do you ask for in the output?
Define the question, its boundaries and whether current sources are required. Ask for citations so you can check the claims.
Source: Prompt configuration by task type
Q9
In a drafting prompt, what do you specify and what do you leave to Claude?
You specify audience, tone and format. Claude finds the phrasing. You control the shape; Claude fills it.
Source: Prompt configuration by task type
Q10
How do you set up a brainstorming chat, and why keep it loose?
Give the goal and the boundaries, then ask for many varied ideas before narrowing. Too many constraints kill the variety you want.
Source: Prompt configuration by task type

D2

Output Evaluation and Validation

Exam weight 21%

Spot hallucination patterns, tune prompts for verifiability, check output with concrete techniques and decide what must never ship without human review.

Q13
Describe in one sentence the hallucination pattern called plausible-but-unsupported claims.
A statement that sounds reasonable and fits the topic but has no basis in the source or in fact. It is the most dangerous kind because nothing about it looks wrong.
Q14
Your prompt implies a preferred answer and Claude agrees a little too readily on a question that should be open. What is this an example of?
Confirmation bias in framing: the way you phrased the question steered the answer.
Q15
What are the three techniques to tune a prompt for verifiability, and how do you implement each?
  1. Permit "I don't know": tell Claude it may say so (works well in a system prompt or Project instructions).
  2. Restrict to provided sources: attach the documents or links and tell Claude to answer only from them.
  3. Require auditable citations: ask for direct quotes from the PDF or traceable links to the exact section of a page.
Q16
Name three ways to check Claude's output.
Quote first, then analyze · Best-of-N comparison · Validate against an authoritative source
Source: Anthropic docs: Reduce hallucinations
Q17
Before Claude analyzes a long document, what should you ask it to do first?
Extract the word-for-word quotes that support the task, then analyze using only those quotes. Anthropic suggests this for documents over ~20k tokens.
Source: Anthropic docs: Reduce hallucinations
Q18
Why does quote-first analysis make Claude's output easier to check?
Each conclusion ties to a pulled quote, so the reasoning and the errors become visible. You can check each quote against the source.
Source: Anthropic docs: Reduce hallucinations
Q19
Best-of-N: after re-running the same request several times, how do you read the results?
Agreement raises confidence. Divergence marks a soft spot that needs a human look (a possible hallucination).
Source: Anthropic docs: Reduce hallucinations
Q20
Why isn't a second Claude response enough to validate a claim that matters?
It is not independent and can repeat the same error. Check against a trusted external reference.
Source: Anthropic docs: "Always validate critical information"
Q21
You're about to act on a figure Claude produced in a spreadsheet. Which in-product aid helps you validate it?
Claude for Excel's cell-level citations, which tie each figure back to its input cells.
Q22
What belongs on the do-not-ship-without-review list? (Decide it in advance and treat it as fixed.)
  • Final client deliverables
  • Audit-critical or financially material calculations
  • Anything with regulated, confidential or highly sensitive data
  • Public or legal communications where a misstatement has lasting consequences
Figure · Four risk thresholds for reviewing AI output
ThresholdWhat to ask
StakesWhat is the cost if this is wrong? High-cost errors demand human review regardless of how confident the output appears.
ReversibilityCan the action be undone? An irreversible step (a sent client deliverable, a filed report) clears a higher bar than a draft you can revise.
AudienceWho sees it? External, executive and regulatory audiences raise the review requirement above internal working drafts.
Regulatory exposureDoes a rule, contract or law govern this? Regulated content carries obligations that AI assistance does not remove.

D3

Platform and Model Selection

Exam weight 12%

Know what a context window holds and how the chat product's limits differ from the model's.

Figure · What fills one request's context window
System promptMessages · files · tool resultsTool definitionsOutputThinking

input + output + reasoning ≤ model window. The chat product (claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini app) may cap you lower than the model's advertised maximum.

Q27
What counts toward a model's context window in a single API request?
Everything sent plus everything generated: system prompt + messages (including tool results and files) + tool definitions + output + thinking tokens.
Source: Anthropic docs: Context windows
Q28
Model context window vs. chat-product context window: which one limits you in the app, and how do they relate?
The product's cap limits you. The model window is the hard ceiling for one request. The product sets its own cap per plan and model, and that cap can sit below the model's maximum.
Source: Anthropic docs; Claude Help Center
Q29
A chat app loses early details in a long conversation even though its model advertises 1M tokens. What should you suspect first?
The product's cap for your plan and model, plus the app trimming old turns (rolling first-in-first-out). The model's advertised max is not what the app gives you.
Source: Anthropic docs: Context windows

When the task genuinely depends on the sensitive specifics you would remove.
